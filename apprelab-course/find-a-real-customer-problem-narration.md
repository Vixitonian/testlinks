## Lesson 1
### Nobody buys a course about your skill. They buy a way out of their problem.

**End state:** the learner can test any course idea with three questions (who, stuck on what, what they'll have) and knows that a narrower idea beats a longer one.

[HOOK]
Here's a strange thing. The best teacher in the room often sells the fewest courses. Not because they teach badly. Because they named the course after what they know, instead of what the buyer needs.

[STORY]
Let me tell you about Kemi. She's an example, not a real person, but you've probably met someone just like her. Kemi is an accountant in Lagos. Ten years of experience. Friends call her when their books are a mess. Every month, someone sends her a voice note. "Kemi, abeg, help me look at this."

So one day she thinks, why not make a course? She spends three weeks of evenings on it. Forty slides. Clean examples. Proper definitions. She calls it Accounting Basics. She posts it on her WhatsApp status. She shares it in two business groups. Then she waits.

[TENSION]
Three people click. One person asks, "Is it for students?" Nobody pays.

Kemi is confused. These are the same people who send her voice notes every month. They clearly need help. So what went wrong?

Here's the thing. None of them ever sent a voice note saying, "Kemi, I want to learn accounting." They said things like, "My shop is always busy, but my money finishes." Or, "I don't know if I'm making profit." Or, "My customer owes me, and I've forgotten how much."

Kemi heard problems. Then she sold a subject. It's like a patient telling a doctor, "My head is pounding," and the doctor handing over a textbook on the human brain. Accurate. Impressive. No help at all.

[FIRST PRINCIPLE]
And that's the whole lesson in one line. Nobody buys a course about your skill. They buy a way out of their problem.

People don't wake up wanting to learn accounting. Or Canva. Or Excel. They wake up stuck. The shop is busy, but the money is gone. The flyer looks cheap. The client hasn't paid. A course is worth paying for when it gets them unstuck.

There's an old marketing line, usually credited to Theodore Levitt, a Harvard professor. People don't want a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch hole. Your skill is the drill. Your learner only cares about the hole. And the better you are at your skill, the easier it is to forget that.

[EXAMPLE]
Now watch what happens when Kemi changes one thing. Same knowledge. Same teacher. New name.

Instead of Accounting Basics, she writes: Know Your Shop's Profit in Thirty Minutes a Week.

Read that as a market trader. Think of Mama Nkechi, who sells fabric in Balogun market. She's another example, not a real person. Mama Nkechi sees Accounting Basics and thinks, that's for university people. She sees Know Your Shop's Profit in Thirty Minutes a Week, and she thinks, wait, that's me.

In one line, the new title tells her three things. This is for me. It fixes what's hurting. And this is what I'll have at the end.

Accounting Basics makes her do the hard part herself. She has to work out how balance sheets will fix her purse problem. Most people won't do that work. They can't picture themselves in it. They don't know what they'll walk away with. So they don't buy. And if they do buy, they don't finish.

The course named after the problem does that work for her.

[SECOND EXAMPLE]
This isn't just about accounting. Think of a graphic designer who wants to teach. Master Canva is a skill. Make a church programme flyer that doesn't look cheap, on your phone, in one evening? That's a way out. Or a public speaking coach. Public Speaking one-oh-one is a skill. Give a five-minute wedding toast without your hands shaking? That's a way out.

Same teacher. Same knowledge. One version describes the teacher. The other describes the learner's life.

[APPLICATION]
So how do you know which one you have? Here's a simple test. Before you build anything, show your course idea to a stranger. From the idea alone, they should be able to answer three questions.

One. Who is it for? A specific person, not anyone.

Two. What are they stuck on today?

Three. What will they have at the end that they don't have now?

Try it on Accounting Basics. Who is it for? It depends. What are they stuck on? Not clear. What will they have? Some knowledge, maybe.

Now try Know Your Shop's Profit in Thirty Minutes a Week. Who? Shop owners. Stuck on what? Not knowing if they're making money. What will they have? Their profit, known, every week.

Here's the rule. If any answer is "it depends" or "everyone," your idea is still a topic. It isn't a course yet.

[PROOF / THE COMMON MISTAKE]
Now, here's where most people go wrong. When a course isn't selling, they think it isn't complete. So they add more. More slides. More modules. A bonus chapter on tax. Kemi almost did this. She was about to add ten more lessons.

That would have made it worse. A broad course with more content is still a broad course. It's just longer. Nobody finishes it, and nobody tells their friends about it.

A course gets better by getting narrower, not longer. Cut the audience down to one kind of person. Cut the promise down to one result. It will feel like you're throwing knowledge away. You're not. You're making it usable.

[ACTION]
So here's your task, right now. Take the course idea you've been carrying around. Write it in one line. Then write the three answers under it. Who is it for? What are they stuck on today? What will they have at the end?

If you wrote "it depends" or "everyone" anywhere, circle it. That circle is where the next lesson starts.

[CLOSE]
Your skill is how you help. Their problem is why they pay.

## Lesson 2
### "Anyone who wants to learn this" is nobody. Pick one person you could call today.

**End state:** the learner has written their person as one sentence (role, situation, what's at stake) and named one real person who fits it.

[HOOK]
Quick question. Who is your course for? If your answer is "anyone who wants to learn this," I have bad news. You just described nobody.

[STORY]
Back to Kemi. Remember, she's an example, not a real person. After her first course flopped, she rewrote her target audience. She wrote: small business owners, aged twenty-five to forty-five. It felt professional. It looked like something from a marketing plan.

Then she sat down to write lesson one. And she froze.

Should she explain things for a woman selling tomatoes on a table? Or a man running a pharmacy with five staff? A tailor who works from home? A fashion brand selling on Instagram? All of them are small business owners. All of them could be thirty years old. And they have completely different problems.

[TENSION]
The pharmacy owner already has an accountant and wants better software. The tomato seller has no laptop and writes nothing down. The Instagram brand loses track of orders in its DMs. One course can't speak to all three. If Kemi tries, every lesson will be too simple for one and too hard for another. Everyone will feel it wasn't quite for them.

That's the trap. Small business owners is a market. You can't talk to a market. And you can't write lessons for one.

[FIRST PRINCIPLE]
You can only write for a person. And a person isn't an age or an income. A person is someone in a specific role, in a specific situation, at a moment when something is at stake.

Two thirty-year-olds can have completely different problems. But two people in the same situation usually share one.

So write your person as one sentence. Their role. The situation they're in. And what's at stake.

[EXAMPLE]
Here's what Kemi wrote the second time.

"Market traders in Balogun who sell on credit and restock every week, and who don't know if they're making money."

Look at what that sentence gives her. The role: market trader. The situation: selling on credit, restocking every week. What's at stake: every week they spend real cash on new stock, without knowing if last week paid for itself.

Now Kemi can see someone. She can see Mama Nkechi, our fabric seller, also an example, not a real person. Mama Nkechi gives cloth on credit to regular customers before Christmas. She buys new stock every Monday. She keeps all the money in one purse. And she honestly doesn't know if last month was good or bad.

Suddenly, writing lesson one is easy. Kemi knows Mama Nkechi has a phone, not a laptop. She knows Mama Nkechi is busy from seven in the morning. She knows the word "ledger" will lose her, but the word "notebook" won't. One clear person answers a hundred small questions Kemi used to guess at. How long should a lesson be? Short enough to finish between customers. Which example should she use? Cloth, credit and Christmas.

[APPLICATION]
So where do you find your person? Good news. You already know them. Look in three places.

First, people who ask you for help. Scroll through your WhatsApp. What question do people keep sending you? The same question from different people is a course waiting to happen. For Kemi, it was "Am I making profit?" She got it every month.

Second, you, three years ago. What did you struggle with before you knew what you know now? You remember what confused you. You remember the words you used back then. That's gold.

Third, your clients' repeated mistakes. What do you fix again and again? If you're a designer, maybe every client sends their logo as a blurry screenshot. If you're a tutor, maybe every parent asks the same thing in exam season. Repeated mistakes point to a real person with a real gap.

[SIGNS YOU PICKED WELL]
How do you know you picked well? Three signs.

One. They already spend time or money trying to fix the problem. Mama Nkechi has tried writing in a notebook. She's asked her son to help. She's thought about downloading an app. She's already fighting it.

Two. You can find ten more like them easily. A WhatsApp group. A market association. A church group. A LinkedIn search. If you can't find ten, you can't sell to ten.

Three. You can talk to one of them this week. Not someday. This week. If you can't reach even one, you'll end up guessing. And guesses make bad courses.

Kemi checks all three. Mama Nkechi is already fighting it. There are hundreds of traders like her in one market. And Kemi can call her tonight.

[PROOF / THE COMMON MISTAKE]
Here's the mistake almost everyone makes. They describe a demographic instead of a situation. Age. Gender. Income. Location. It looks smart in a plan. But it tells you nothing about what hurts.

Women aged twenty-five to thirty-five in Lagos could be a banker, a nursing mother, a student and a hairdresser. Four people. Four different problems.

Hairdressers who run a salon alone, and lose money when customers miss appointments? That's one person. You can call her. You can help her.

The narrower person feels risky. You worry you'll lose everyone else. But a course for everyone gets bought by no one. A course for one clear person gets bought by that person, and by everyone who recognises themselves in her.

[ACTION]
Your task now. Write your person in one sentence, using the pattern. Their role, the situation they're in, and what's at stake.

Then write down the name of one real person who fits it. Someone whose number is already in your phone. You'll need them in the next lesson.

[CLOSE]
Don't build for a market. Build for one person you could call today.

## Lesson 3
### A problem worth a course is one they already feel, described in their own words.

**End state:** the learner has asked their person three questions about a real moment and written one problem statement in that person's words, checked as frequent, costly and already being fought.

[HOOK]
If you ask people what their problem is, most of them will tell you the wrong thing. Not because they're lying. Because they describe how it feels, not what's actually broken.

[STORY]
Kemi, still our example, not a real person, finally calls Mama Nkechi. She asks, "What's your biggest problem in the business?"

Mama Nkechi laughs. "My sister, money. I'm always broke."

Kemi nods. She thinks, okay, a course on saving money. Then she stops. That's not something she can fix. Always broke could mean a hundred things. Prices too low. The wrong stock. Too much credit. Somebody borrowing from the till. Kemi can't build a course on a feeling. And she can't write a lesson called "Stop Being Broke."

[TENSION]
This is where most course creators go wrong. They take the first answer and run. "I'm always broke" becomes a course on financial freedom. "My designs look cheap" becomes a course on design theory. "Clients don't reply" becomes a course on communication. All too broad. All built on symptoms.

A symptom is what the person feels. The problem is the specific thing they can't do that causes the feeling. Treat the symptom, and nothing changes.

[FIRST PRINCIPLE]
So here's the idea. You don't find the real problem by asking for opinions. You find it by asking about real moments.

Opinions are cheap. Ask "Would you buy a course on this?" and everybody says yes, just to be polite. Real moments are hard to fake. Ask "What happened last Tuesday?" and there's only one true answer.

Rob Fitzpatrick wrote a short book about this, called The Mom Test. His point is simple. Ask about what people actually did in the past, not what they think they'd do in the future. Even your mum can't fool you about last week.

[EXAMPLE]
So Kemi tries again. This time she uses three questions.

First. "Tell me about the last time this went wrong. What were you trying to do?"

Mama Nkechi thinks. "Last month. I went to restock on Monday. I counted the purse and thought I had enough. I bought Ankara for almost two hundred thousand naira. Then on Thursday, my shop rent was due, and the money wasn't there."

Second. "What did you try?"

"I tried writing my sales in a notebook. But I stopped after two weeks. And the house money and shop money are in the same purse. When my children need school fees, I take from it."

Third. "What did it cost you?"

"I had to borrow from my cousin. It was embarrassing. And I couldn't sleep that week."

Now look at what Kemi has. Not "always broke." Something specific. Mama Nkechi can't tell whether she made a profit, because shop money and house money share one purse. So she restocks with money she doesn't really have.

That's a problem Kemi can solve. And listen to the words. Same purse. House money. Restock. Those are Mama Nkechi's words. They'll go into Kemi's title and lessons, so the course sounds like it was written for her. Because it was.

[APPLICATION]
Now write it down as a problem statement. The pattern is: this person can't do a specific thing, when this situation happens, so this is the cost.

Kemi's version: "Market traders can't tell whether they made a profit this month, because shop money and house money share one purse, so they restock with money they don't have."

Then check that it's worth a course. A problem is worth solving when it passes three tests.

Is it frequent? It happens every week or every month, not once in a lifetime. Mama Nkechi restocks every Monday. Pass.

Is it costly? They can name what it costs. Money, time, a client, sleep, or face. Mama Nkechi named a loan, a sleepless week and embarrassment. Pass.

Is it already being fought? They've tried something that didn't work. She tried a notebook and quit. Pass.

If a problem fails these tests, people won't pay to fix it. If it passes all three, they're already looking for help. You're just showing up with the answer they were already looking for.

[SECOND EXAMPLE]
Let's do one more, fast. A graphic designer asks a church media volunteer about the last flyer. The volunteer says, "Saturday night, the pastor wanted a flyer by morning. I used a template, but the text was everywhere, and he said it looked rough." What did you try? "YouTube videos, but they're all done on laptops." What did it cost? "I was up until two in the morning, and he still wasn't happy."

The problem isn't "bad at design." It's this. Church volunteers can't make a clean flyer on their phone at short notice, so they lose sleep and face.

[PROOF / THE COMMON MISTAKE]
The common mistake is writing the problem in your expert language. Kemi's first draft said "poor working-capital management." That's true. It's also useless. Mama Nkechi would never say it, and she'd never click on it.

If your person wouldn't say it, rewrite it. Use their words, even if they feel too simple. Simple is what makes them say, "That's me."

[ACTION]
Your task. Message or call the person you named in the last lesson. Ask the three questions. The last time it went wrong. What they tried. What it cost. Write down their exact words.

Then write one problem statement. This person can't do this, when this happens, so this is the cost. Check that it's frequent, costly and already being fought.

[CLOSE]
Listen for the moment, not the opinion. The real problem is always hiding in last week.

## Lesson 4
### "They will understand it" is not an outcome. Something they can show you is.

**End state:** the learner has written a promise with a visible result that fits their person's tools, time and money, sized for one to two weeks, plus the proof they'll hand in.

[HOOK]
"By the end of this course, you'll understand bookkeeping." Sounds fine, right? Here's the problem. How would you ever know if it worked?

[STORY]
Kemi, our example, not a real person, now has her person and her problem. Mama Nkechi can't tell if she's making profit, because shop money and house money share one purse. So Kemi writes her course promise. "By the end, you will understand bookkeeping and feel confident with your finances."

She shows it to Mama Nkechi. Mama Nkechi reads it, smiles politely, and asks, "Okay. But what will I have?"

[TENSION]
Kemi has no answer. Understanding doesn't come with anything you can hold. Neither does feeling confident. You can't see understanding. You can't check confidence. Mama Nkechi could finish the whole course and still not know if she made money last month. And she'd have no way to tell.

Neither would Kemi. A promise you can't check is a promise you can't keep.

Think about how you judge anyone else's work. When you take your car to a mechanic, you don't ask whether they understand engines. You ask one thing. Does the car start? When you pay a tailor, you don't want them to feel confident with fabric. You want the dress, ready, fitting, on Saturday. We judge everyone by what they hand us. Your learners will judge your course the same way.

[FIRST PRINCIPLE]
Here's the idea underneath. An outcome is real only if the learner can show it to you. Something they can produce, show or use.

So use verbs you can check. Write. Send. Set up. Build. Record. Close. Price. And drop the soft ones. Understand. Know. Learn about. Be confident with. You can't see any of them.

[EXAMPLE]
So Kemi rewrites. "Understand bookkeeping" becomes "a one-page profit record for last month, kept in a notebook or a WhatsApp note."

Now Mama Nkechi can picture it. One page. Last month. In a notebook she already owns, or in WhatsApp, which is already on her phone. At the end, she'll have a page that says: this is what came in, this is what went out, this is what's left. She can show it to her husband. She can look at it before she restocks on Monday.

And Kemi can check it. Either the page exists, or it doesn't.

[FIT IT TO THEIR SITUATION]
But a visible outcome isn't enough. It has to fit the person's situation. Remember the sentence from lesson two: role, situation, what's at stake. That situation sets the limits.

Ask three questions. Tools: do they have a laptop, or only a phone? Only free apps? Time: twenty minutes a day? Only weekends? Money: can they pay for software?

Kemi asks Mama Nkechi. Phone only. Twenty minutes, maybe, after the shop closes. No money for apps. So the outcome has to live in a notebook or a WhatsApp note. Nothing else.

Imagine Kemi had promised, "Set up your accounts in QuickBooks." It's a real outcome. You can check it. But Mama Nkechi has no laptop, and she's not paying a monthly subscription. Brilliant teaching, wrong outcome. It fails before lesson one.

An outcome that ignores the situation fails, however good the teaching.

[SIZE IT]
Next, size it. A micro-course has three to eight lessons, with a task in every one. The outcome should be reachable in one to two weeks.

If it needs months, pick the first result on the way. "A steady, profitable business" is too big. That's a life goal. "Last month's profit, known" is the first step toward it. It's small. It's real. And once Mama Nkechi has it, she'll want the next step. Maybe that's Kemi's second course.

Kemi was tempted to promise a full year of records. Then she thought it through. Twelve months of old receipts, from a woman who never kept any? Mama Nkechi would quit in week one. One month is enough to prove it works. And it's enough to change what she does next Monday.

[SECOND EXAMPLE]
Same idea, another world. A fitness coach writes, "Understand healthy eating." Rewrite it: "A seven-day meal plan using food from your local market, within a budget you set." A singing teacher writes, "Learn to sing better." Rewrite it: "A one-minute recording of one song, sung in key, that you're happy to send to your choir leader." Each one, you can hold or hear.

[PROOF SELLS]
There's a bonus to visible outcomes. Proof sells the next learner. When someone finishes a course and can show what they made, they tell people. "Look what I did."

This is built into Apprelab. Every course there ends with a real task and a verified badge. Learners share that badge on WhatsApp and LinkedIn. And each badge links back to the course. So every person who finishes advertises your course for you.

Think about that for Kemi. Mama Nkechi finishes, gets her badge, and posts it in her traders' WhatsApp group. Other traders see it. Many of them have the same purse problem. Some of them click.

[PROOF / THE COMMON MISTAKE]
The common mistake is ending a course with "congratulations." A nice message, a thank you, and nothing to show. The learner feels good for a minute. Then nothing changes, and nobody else hears about it.

End with proof instead. Decide now what your learner will hand in. That becomes the task in your last lesson. For Kemi, it's simple. A photo of Mama Nkechi's one-page profit record for last month.

[ACTION]
Your task. Write your promise using this pattern. "By the end, you will be able to," then the visible result, then their situation. Under it, write one line: what will they hand in to prove it?

Check your verb. If it's "understand" or "know," rewrite it until you could see the result sitting on a table.

[CLOSE]
If you can't see it, you can't promise it.

## Lesson 5
### Person + problem + situation + outcome = your course. Now write it down.

**End state:** the learner has a one-page course plan (person, problem, situation, promise, title, three to eight lessons each with a task, last task as proof) and has shown it to their person.

[HOOK]
Most people think building a course starts with lesson one. It doesn't. It starts with the last lesson. You build it backwards.

[STORY]
Kemi, our example, not a real person, now has four notes on her table.

Her person: market traders in Balogun who sell on credit, restock every week, and don't know if they're making money.

Her problem, in Mama Nkechi's words: shop money and house money share one purse, so she restocks with money she doesn't have.

The situation: phone only, no laptop, busy from seven in the morning, no money for software.

And the outcome: a one-page profit record for last month, in a notebook or a WhatsApp note.

Three weeks ago, she had forty slides called Accounting Basics. Now she has something much better. But it's still four separate notes. It isn't a course yet.

[TENSION]
Here's where people get stuck again. They have all the pieces. Then they sit down to write lessons, and the old habit comes back. They start with "What is accounting?" Then "The history of bookkeeping." Then "Types of accounts." Before long, they're back to forty slides. Every one interesting. Most of them useless to the person.

Kemi felt the pull too. Her first draft of lesson one was called Introduction to Financial Records. She read it aloud and heard her old course coming back.

[FIRST PRINCIPLE]
The fix is one simple idea. Person, plus problem, plus situation, plus outcome, equals your course. Everything else is decoration.

Your title names the outcome. Your lessons are the steps to reach it. And every step must move the person closer. If it doesn't, it goes.

[EXAMPLE: THE TITLE]
Start with the title. The pattern is: get this result, plus for this person or situation.

Kemi's first try is Bookkeeping for Traders. Better, but it still names a skill. It tells Mama Nkechi who it's for, but not what she'll have. So she tries again.

Kemi writes: Know Your Shop's Profit in Thirty Minutes a Week.

Here's another, for a different creator: Get Your First Ten Instagram Orders Without Paid Ads.

Now test it. Would your person read it and say, "That's me"? Kemi reads hers to Mama Nkechi. Mama Nkechi says, "When can I start?" Test passed. If your person needs it explained, it's still a topic.

[APPLICATION: THE LESSONS]
Now the lessons. Work backwards from the outcome. Ask: what's the very first thing she must be able to do? Then the next? Then the next?

Kemi works it out loud. To have a one-page profit record, Mama Nkechi needs to know what came in and what went out. To know what came in, she must record each day's sales. Before any of that, she must separate shop money from house money, or the numbers mean nothing. And she needs to track who owes her, because credit sales aren't cash yet.

So the lessons fall into place.

Lesson one: split the purse. The task: set up two envelopes, one for the shop, one for the house.

Lesson two: record today's sales in two minutes. The task: send yourself a WhatsApp note with today's total.

Lesson three: track who owes you. The task: list every customer on credit, and how much.

Lesson four: write down what went out. The task: list last week's stock, rent and transport.

Lesson five: your one-page profit record. The task: send a photo of last month's page.

Five lessons. A task in every one. And the last task is the proof Kemi chose in lesson four. That's a micro-course.

[WHAT NOT TO DO]
Now look at what Kemi cut. Double-entry bookkeeping. Balance sheets. Tax. Depreciation. She knows all of it. Some of it is fascinating. None of it moves Mama Nkechi toward her one page. So it's gone. Kemi writes those topics on a separate page called "later." Nothing is wasted. If her learners ask for more, that page becomes her next course.

That's the hardest part, cutting good knowledge. But it's what keeps the course micro. And it's why people finish.

[YOU JUST DID IT]
Here's something you might not have noticed. This course followed the same method. One person: you, with real knowledge but no clear course. One problem: ideas that are too broad. One situation: limited time, and no course-building experience. One outcome: a plan for a real course. Five lessons, with a task in each. Now comes your final task. The proof.

[PUBLISH]
When your plan is ready, it fits straight into the Creator Studio on Apprelab.com. It asks for exactly this. One outcome, three to eight lessons, and a task in every lesson. Paste your plan or your notes into its AI course builder. It drafts each lesson, the quick checks and the tasks. When learners hand in their work, they get instant AI feedback. You can publish it free, or set a price and earn from every sale.

[PROOF / THE COMMON MISTAKE]
The common mistake at this stage is keeping a lesson because you love it. A great story. A clever framework. A chapter you worked hard on. If it doesn't move your person toward the outcome, it slows them down. Slow courses don't get finished. And unfinished courses don't get shared.

[ACTION]
Your final task. On one page, write your course plan. Your person, in one sentence. The problem, in their words. The situation, with its limits. The promise, starting with "By the end, you will be able to." A title. Then three to eight lessons, each with one task. Make the last task the proof. Don't make it pretty. A page of honest notes is enough.

Then send it to the person you called in lesson three. Ask them one question. "Is this you?"

[CLOSE]
Kemi started with a skill. She ended with a course Mama Nkechi asked to start. Now it's your turn.
